Reading a stock chart, from zero

Nine lessons, no jargon. Candles and trend structure first, then volume, moving averages, momentum, patterns — and the part that actually protects your money, which is risk.

You said the thing that worries you is: it seems like an easy way to lose money, and there's a lot of hype around it — I don't want to lose a lot of money and get stressed out.

That's the correct instinct, and it's the one this page is built around. Trading is an easy way to lose money — mostly because people act before they can read what's in front of them. So there is nothing to buy here and no signals to follow. This is just learning to read. Reading costs nothing, carries no risk, and is the part the hype always skips.

One promise up front, since this is the half of the subject that attracts the most nonsense: technical analysis is not a prediction machine, and no indicator on this page tells you what happens next. What it does is describe the current balance of buyers and sellers, and give you a specific price at which an idea is provably wrong. That second part is the whole of your protection.

01

What one candle is telling you

A stock chart is usually drawn as a row of candles. Each candle covers one slice of time — one day on a daily chart, one hour on an hourly chart — and it records four prices from that slice.

Take a made-up example. Apple AAPL on a daily chart. During that one day:

  • Open — the price at the start of the day. Say $180.
  • High — the highest price anyone paid all day. $186.
  • Low — the lowest price anyone paid all day. $178.
  • Close — the price at the end of the day. $184.

The thick part is the body: it stretches from the open to the close. The thin lines poking out of the top and bottom are the wicks — they reach out to the high and the low. If the close is above the open, the candle is drawn green (an up candle). If the close is below the open, it's red (a down candle).

Wicks matter more than beginners expect. A long wick means price went somewhere and got rejected — buyers or sellers pushed it there, and it came straight back. A candle with a long top wick and a small body says "someone tried to push AAPL to $186 and it didn't stick."

Why this answers your worry

Hype talks in stories — "this stock is going to the moon." A candle is not a story. It's four numbers that already happened. Learning to read them is how you replace someone else's excitement with your own observation.

02

Market structure: what the chart is doing

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 01 to open this.

One candle is a moment. Zoom out and the candles form a shape, and that shape has a name. There are only three, and every stock chart you ever look at is doing one of them.

To see it, ignore the individual candles and look only at the turning points — the peaks (swing highs) and the dips (swing lows).

  • Uptrend — each peak is higher than the last peak, and each dip is higher than the last dip. Higher highs and higher lows. Buyers are in control.
  • Downtrend — each peak is lower than the last, and each dip is lower than the last. Lower highs and lower lows. Sellers are in control.
  • Range — the peaks stop at roughly the same level and the dips stop at roughly the same level. Nobody's in control. Price is going sideways.

Two practical notes. First, structure depends on the timeframe: AAPL can be in an uptrend on the weekly chart and a downtrend on the hourly at the same time, and both are true. Second, a trend doesn't end because it "feels high." It ends when the pattern breaks — an uptrend is over when price makes a lower low, not before.

Why this answers your worry

Most of the money beginners lose is lost buying a stock that's already making lower highs and lower lows, because a headline said it was cheap. Structure gives you one calm question to ask before anything else: which of the three is this? Sometimes the honest answer is "sideways and unclear", and the correct response to that is to do nothing.

03

Support and resistance: floors and ceilings

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 02 to open this.

Now the last piece of the basics. Price doesn't wander randomly — it keeps reacting at the same prices, over and over. Those prices have names.

  • Support is a floor: a price where falls keep stopping, because enough buyers show up there. Say MSFT drops to about $400 three separate times and bounces each time — $400 is support.
  • Resistance is a ceiling: a price where rallies keep stalling, because enough sellers show up there. If MSFT pushes toward $440 and gets knocked back twice, $440 is resistance.

Why do they exist? Nothing mystical. Lots of people remember that price. Some are waiting to buy where they missed out; some are waiting to sell where they broke even. Their orders sit there and price reacts.

Two things to hold on to. Levels are zones, not exact prices — MSFT support at "$400" really means roughly $398–$402. And when a level finally breaks, it often flips role: a ceiling that price climbs above tends to become the floor on the way back down, which is the move drawn on the right of that chart.

Why this answers your worry

This is the piece that most directly protects your money. Levels give you a place where you'd be provably wrong. If you thought $400 was the floor and price closes well below it, the idea was wrong — that's information, not a disaster, and it's a decision you made in advance rather than in a panic. Deciding where you're wrong before you're in is most of the difference between trading and stress. Lesson 09 turns that into arithmetic.

04

Timeframes: the same chart, three answers

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 03 to open this.

You've now got the three basics: candles, structure, levels. Everything from here refines them — starting with the question that has to be settled before any of it means anything. Which timeframe am I looking at, and does it match how long I intend to hold?

A chart's timeframe is how much time each candle covers. On a weekly chart each candle is a week; on a 15-minute chart each candle is fifteen minutes. Lesson 02 mentioned this in passing — here's what it actually looks like. Change the timeframe and the same stock can be in an uptrend, a downtrend and a range simultaneously, and all three readings are correct.

The working rule is top-down. Start on the timeframe well above the one you intend to act on, establish the trend there, and let it set your bias. Then drop down for detail. What you must not do is form a view on the weekly chart and then let a scary 5-minute candle talk you out of it — that candle was always going to be there, and it isn't information about your idea.

Why this answers your worry

Decide your timeframe before you open the chart, not after. Choosing it afterwards is how people end up justifying a position they already hold by scrolling until they find a timeframe that agrees with them — which is stress dressed up as analysis.

05

Volume: how much conviction is behind the move

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 04 to open this.

Volume is the number of shares traded in each period, drawn as bars under the price. Price tells you where; volume tells you how many people cared.

The single most useful application is at a level — exactly the levels from lesson 03. When price finally pushes through a resistance that has held several times, the volume on that push is your conviction check. A breakout on heavy volume means real participation: a lot of buyers were willing to pay above the old ceiling. A breakout on thin volume means very few people were involved, and those tend to fall back inside the range.

A few readings worth knowing:

  • Rising price on rising volume — the trend has participation behind it.
  • Rising price on steadily falling volume — the move is running out of new buyers. Not a sell signal, but a reason to stop adding.
  • A huge volume spike after a long move — often the last rush of people arriving late. Frequently marks a turning point rather than a continuation.
  • Very low volume overall — thin stocks move erratically, cost more to get in and out of, and are far easier to push around. Beginners should avoid them.

One important caveat: volume is much more reliable on stocks and futures than on some other markets. Spot foreign exchange has no central exchange, so the "volume" your platform shows is only what one broker saw, not the whole market.

Why this answers your worry

Ask "who else was involved?" before believing any move. A price that jumps 8% on almost no volume is not the same event as one that jumps 8% on the heaviest day of the year, even though both look identical if you only read the price — and the first is exactly the kind of move that gets hyped.

06

Moving averages: the trend with the noise removed

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 05 to open this.

A moving average is the average closing price over the last N periods, recalculated each period and drawn as a line. That's the entire idea. A 50-day moving average on a daily chart is simply the average of the last 50 closes.

It does one job well: it strips out the day-to-day jitter so the structure you learned in lesson 02 becomes visible at a glance. The trade-off is built in and unavoidable — an average of the past always lags the present. A long average is smooth but slow; a short one is responsive but noisy. There's no setting that avoids this, which is why arguments about the "best" length are mostly wasted breath.

Three legitimate uses, in order of usefulness:

  • Trend filter. Price above a rising 200-day average is a different environment from price below a falling one. Many people use nothing more than this and are better off for it.
  • Dynamic support and resistance. In a healthy trend, pullbacks often stall around the rising average. Treat it as a zone, exactly like the horizontal levels in lesson 03.
  • Crossovers. A fast average crossing above a slow one (the "golden cross") or below it (the "death cross"). These are famous and genuinely lagging — by the time they trigger, a good part of the move has happened. They describe a change that already occurred; they don't call it in advance.
Where this goes wrong

Moving averages are useless in a sideways market. Price chops back and forth across the line, generating cross after cross, each one wrong. This is the standard way beginners lose money with indicators: applying a trend tool in the absence of a trend. Check the structure first — if lesson 02 says it's a range, put the averages away.

On the endless SMA-versus-EMA debate: a simple moving average weights every period equally; an exponential one weights recent periods more heavily, so it turns faster. Pick one and stay consistent. The difference between them is far smaller than the difference between having a plan and not having one.

07

Momentum: how hard price is being pushed

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 06 to open this.

Momentum indicators measure the speed of price movement rather than its direction. The useful question they answer is: is this move still accelerating, or is it running on fumes?

RSI — the relative strength index

RSI compares the size of recent gains to the size of recent losses over a lookback period (14 by default) and scales the result from 0 to 100. High readings mean gains have dominated recently; low readings mean losses have.

The conventional labels are "overbought" above 70 and "oversold" below 30, and this is where almost every beginner goes wrong. Overbought does not mean sell. In a strong uptrend RSI can sit above 70 for weeks while price keeps climbing — that's what strength looks like. Selling every 70 print in a bull market is a reliable way to miss the entire move.

The more informative use is divergence: price makes a higher high, but RSI makes a lower high. Buyers got price to a new peak with noticeably less force behind it. That's a warning about the health of the move, not a signal to reverse — divergences can persist for a long time before anything happens, and plenty resolve by simply going away.

MACD — two averages, plotted as a spread

MACD is the gap between a fast and a slow exponential average, drawn as a line, with an average of itself (the signal line) on top and their difference as a histogram. In plain terms it measures whether the fast average is pulling away from the slow one or closing in on it. Widening histogram bars mean the move is accelerating; shrinking bars mean it's decelerating even while price still rises.

It shares every weakness of moving averages, because it's made of them: reliable-ish in trends, noisy garbage in ranges.

Why this answers your worry

Indicators are derived from price. They cannot know anything price doesn't already contain — they only reshape it into something easier to read. This is why stacking eight of them on one chart adds no information: they're all reading the same numbers. Two, understood properly, beat eight glanced at. Anyone selling you a proprietary indicator is selling you rearranged arithmetic.

08

Patterns: what they actually represent

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 07 to open this.

Chart patterns have exotic names and a reputation for mysticism they don't deserve. Every one of them is a picture of the same two things you already know from lesson 03: a level that keeps holding, and what happens when it stops holding.

Read them as stories about supply and demand rather than as shapes to spot:

  • Double top. Buyers tried twice to get above a price and were beaten both times. When price then breaks the low between the two attempts (the neckline), the people who bought expecting a third try are now offside.
  • Double bottom. The mirror image. Sellers failed twice at the same floor, and the break of the intervening high says buyers have taken control.
  • Consolidation — flags, triangles, boxes. A tight sideways drift after a strong move. Nobody is forcing the issue; the market is digesting. These usually resolve in the direction of the move that preceded them, though "usually" is doing real work in that sentence.
  • Head and shoulders. A double top with an extra, higher middle peak. Same story: a series of failures at the top, then a neckline break.

Three things nobody tells beginners about patterns

  • Patterns fail constantly. A "breakout" that closes back inside the range within a day or two is a false breakout, and it's common enough that experienced traders often wait for the retest rather than chase the first move.
  • You will see patterns that aren't there. Human pattern-matching is aggressive and works on random data too. If you have to squint, it isn't a pattern.
  • The name adds nothing. Knowing it's called a "bull pennant" doesn't improve the outcome. Knowing where you'd be wrong does.
Where this goes wrong

The retrospective trap: on a chart of the past, every pattern that worked is obvious and every one that failed is invisible, because you weren't looking for it. Any pattern only counts if you named it on the right-hand edge of the chart, before the outcome was known. That's a completely different exercise, and it's far harder. It's also why screenshots of perfect setups prove nothing.

09

Risk: the only part that protects you

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 08 to open this.

Everything above this lesson is analysis. This lesson is the one that decides whether you're still solvent in a year, and it's the direct answer to the worry you started with. If you take one section from this page, take this one.

The reason risk management belongs in a technical analysis course is that charts are what make it possible. A level from lesson 03 gives you a specific price at which your idea is demonstrably wrong — and once you have that, you can size the position so that being wrong costs a known, survivable amount.

The three numbers, in order

  • The stop. A price where the reason you're in has stopped being true — usually just beyond a support level or a swing low. Not "a number I'm comfortable losing", and never a mental note.
  • The risk per share. Entry minus stop. In the diagram, $3.
  • The position size. Decide the maximum you'll lose on any single idea — commonly 1% of the account — and divide. $100 of risk ÷ $3 per share = 33 shares. That's the answer, whatever your enthusiasm says.

Expressing everything in R — multiples of your risk — makes results comparable across positions of different sizes, and it takes the money out of the emotional foreground. A loss is "minus one R", not "minus £300 I was going to spend on something".

Why the ratio matters more than the hit rate

You do not need to be right most of the time. With a consistent 2R target and a 1R stop, being right 40% of the time still comes out ahead over many attempts: four wins at +2R is +8R, six losses at −1R is −6R, net +2R before costs. Being right 70% of the time with losses twice the size of your wins loses money. Hit rate alone tells you nothing.

The three ways beginners actually lose

Not from picking bad charts. From position sizing on excitement — one oversized position that wipes out a year of careful ones. From moving the stop when price approaches it, converting a planned small loss into an unplanned large one. And from revenge trading — trying to win it straight back after a loss, at exactly the moment your judgement is worst. All three are decisions made after entering. All three are prevented by deciding beforehand and writing it down.

Two more practical points. Costs are real: spreads, commissions and slippage come out of every result, and strategies that look profitable on paper often aren't after them. And leverage — trading with borrowed money, including CFDs and most crypto perpetuals — multiplies both directions. It is not a way to make a small account grow faster; it is a way to make a small mistake final.
