What a company is actually worth

Fundamental analysis is reading the business behind the ticker: what it earns, what it owns, what it owes, and whether the price being asked for it makes any sense.

There are two honest ways to look at a stock, and they answer different questions.

Fundamental analysis asks what is this business worth? Technical analysis asks what is price doing right now? Neither is the "real" one. This page is the first question. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy anything — it's how to read a company, which costs nothing and risks nothing.

01

A share is a slice of a business

A share is not a lottery ticket with a company logo on it. It is legal ownership of a fraction of a real business — its profits, its assets, its debts and its problems.

Say a fictional company, Northwind Coffee, has 100 million shares outstanding and each one trades at $20. Multiply the two and you get the market capitalisation: $2 billion. That is the price the market is putting on the whole company today.

Which sets up the only question fundamental analysis really asks:

  • Price is what the market charges. It's a fact, visible on any screen.
  • Value is what the business is worth, based on the cash it can produce over its lifetime. It's an estimate, and yours will differ from mine.

You never get to know value exactly. The useful goal is a rough range you can defend, and then noticing when price is far outside it. A $2 billion price tag on a business earning $200 million a year is a very different proposition from the same price tag on a business earning $5 million a year — even though both charts might look identical.

The habit this builds

Whenever someone quotes you a share price, it means nothing on its own. A $900 share isn't "expensive" and a $3 share isn't "cheap" — those are just the results of how many slices the pie was cut into. Price per share is meaningless until you pair it with earnings per share.

02

The three financial statements

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 01 to open this.

Every public company publishes the same three reports every quarter. They are free, and between them they tell you almost everything a beginner needs. Each answers a different question.

The income statement — a funnel from sales to profit

It starts with everything that came in the door and subtracts its way down. Each subtraction has a name, and the leftover after each one has a name too.

Divide any of those leftovers by revenue and you get a margin. Gross margin 58%, operating margin 22%, net margin 16%. Divide net income by the share count and you get earnings per share (EPS) — the single most quoted number in investing, and the one that feeds most valuation ratios.

The balance sheet — what it owns and owes, today

One equation holds always: assets = liabilities + equity. Assets are what the company controls (cash, inventory, buildings, patents). Liabilities are claims other people have on it (debt, bills due, deferred revenue). What's left over — equity — belongs to shareholders. That's you.

The thing to look for as a beginner is simple: does it have more cash than debt, or the other way round? Debt isn't evil — it's how most real businesses grow — but debt is a promise with a date on it, and a company that can't meet it doesn't get a second opinion.

The cash flow statement — the honest one

Net income involves judgement calls: when to book a sale, how fast to depreciate a machine. Cash is harder to argue with. Operating cash flow is the money the business actually generated. Subtract capital expenditure (the money spent on equipment and buildings just to keep going and grow) and you get free cash flow — cash genuinely available to pay down debt, buy back shares, pay a dividend, or reinvest.

If profits are rising for years but free cash flow is flat or negative, that's the single most useful red flag an amateur can spot. It usually means the "profit" is stuck in unpaid invoices or unsold inventory.

The habit this builds

Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact. When a company's story and its cash flow statement disagree, believe the cash flow statement.

The handful of numbers that actually matter

There are hundreds of metrics. You need about seven. Here they are, with what each one is really asking.

Revenue growththis year ÷ last year − 1Is the business getting bigger, and is that speeding up or slowing down?
Gross margingross profit ÷ revenueHow much pricing power does it have over its own product?
Operating marginoperating profit ÷ revenueIs it disciplined about its own overheads?

Return on equitynet income ÷ equityHow well does it turn shareholders' money into profit?

Two of those deserve extra attention because beginners routinely miss them.

Margins tell you what kind of business it is

A supermarket might run a 3% net margin and a software company 30% — that isn't one being "better run", it's two different businesses. What matters is the direction over time and how it compares to direct competitors. Margins quietly falling for six straight quarters is a story; a single number in isolation is not.

Share count: the dilution most people never check

If profits grow 10% but the company issues 12% more shares, your slice of those profits actually shrank. Companies issue shares to pay staff, to fund acquisitions, or to raise cash. It isn't automatically bad — but it is a cost, and it doesn't appear as one on the income statement.

The habit this builds

Always read metrics as a trend, not a snapshot. One quarter is noise. Three years of direction is information. Most company reports helpfully print several years side by side for exactly this reason.

04

Valuation: is the price sensible?

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 03 to open this.

A great business at a terrible price is a bad investment. Valuation ratios exist to put price and performance on the same scale so you can compare a $2bn company with a $200bn one.

Price-to-earnings (P/E), the one everyone quotes

Share price divided by earnings per share. If Northwind Coffee trades at $20 and earned $1.00 per share last year, its P/E is 20. The most useful way to say that out loud: you're paying twenty years of current earnings for one share. Flip it upside down and you get the earnings yield — 1 ÷ 20 = 5% — which you can compare directly to what a savings account or a government bond pays.

A high P/E is not automatically "expensive" and a low one is not automatically "cheap". A P/E of 40 says the market expects earnings to grow quickly; if they do, it was cheap. A P/E of 6 often belongs to a business the market thinks is shrinking — the famous value trap, where the ratio stays low forever because the earnings keep falling underneath it.

The rest of the toolkit

RatioUse it whenWatch out for
P/EThe company is reliably profitableUseless if earnings are negative or a one-off distorted them
Forward P/EYou want price against next year's expected earningsIt's built on analyst forecasts, which are often too optimistic
P/SYoung or unprofitable companies with real revenueIgnores whether those sales ever become profit
EV/EBITDAComparing companies with very different debt loadsEBITDA flatters companies with heavy equipment costs
P/BBanks and asset-heavy businessesMeaningless for software, where the value isn't on the books
FCF yieldYou want the cash-based version of earnings yieldLumpy capex can make a single year misleading

A ratio on its own is a number with no meaning. It becomes information only when you compare it to something:

  • Its own history. Has this company usually traded at 15× and it's now at 30×? Why?
  • Its direct competitors. Compare a retailer to a retailer, not to a chip designer.
  • Its growth rate. A P/E of 30 for a business growing 30% a year is a different proposition than a P/E of 30 for one growing 3%.
  • The risk-free rate. If a government bond pays 5% with no risk, a 4% earnings yield with plenty of risk needs an argument behind it.
Where this goes wrong

Cheap is not the same as good, and good is not the same as cheap. The two mistakes are symmetrical: buying a declining business because the ratio looks low, and paying any price for a wonderful business because the story is exciting. You need both halves of the judgement.

05

The part that isn't a number

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 04 to open this.

Numbers describe what already happened. Whether it keeps happening depends on things you have to reason about rather than calculate.

The moat

High profits attract competitors the way an open window attracts flies. A moat is whatever stops them. The durable kinds are worth knowing by name:

  • Switching costs — moving off the product is painful. Enterprise software, banking, payroll systems.
  • Network effects — each new user makes the product better for existing ones. Marketplaces, exchanges, social platforms.
  • Cost advantage — it can genuinely produce cheaper than anyone else, through scale or location or process.
  • Intangibles — brands people pay a premium for, patents, regulatory licences.

A useful test: if a well-funded competitor launched an identical product tomorrow at a 20% lower price, what happens? If the honest answer is "most customers leave", there's no moat, and today's high margins are borrowed rather than owned.

Management and incentives

Read what the company said it would do three years ago, then check what it did. That comparison tells you more than any interview. Then check how the people running it are paid — if bonuses depend on revenue growth alone, expect acquisitions and price cuts; if they depend on return on capital, expect discipline.

What can go wrong

Write the bear case yourself before you own anything. Customer concentration (one client is 40% of sales), regulatory risk, a single supplier, debt that must be refinanced next year at higher rates, technology that could obsolete the product. If you can't articulate what would make you wrong, you don't understand the position well enough to hold it through a bad month.

The habit this builds

Fundamental analysis is slow on purpose. Nothing in this lesson changes hour to hour, which means there's never a reason to rush a decision. If an opportunity requires you to act before you've finished reading, it isn't an opportunity — it's pressure.

The ten-minute first pass

Before any deep work, run this on any company. It rules out most of them fast:

  • Can you explain in one sentence how it makes money?
  • Revenue over five years — growing, flat, or shrinking?
  • Is it profitable, and are margins going up or down?
  • Is free cash flow positive and roughly tracking net income?
  • More cash than debt, or a debt load it can comfortably service?
  • Share count — rising or falling over five years?
  • What's the P/E versus its own history and its competitors?
  • What's the one thing that would break this business?
