Course · Part two of three · Accounting

Reading the three statements

Eight lessons on the accounting a private investor actually needs: what each statement says, how the three lock into one another, and where the judgement calls are hiding.

Part one made a claim and then moved on: profit is an opinion, cash is a fact. This page is where that claim gets proved.

Accounting has a reputation for being both dull and impenetrable, and it earns about half of that. The dull half is real. The impenetrable half is mostly vocabulary — perhaps forty terms, each of which means something ordinary. There is no maths here beyond addition, subtraction and division.

What you get for the effort is the ability to check a story against the numbers. Almost every company that has gone badly wrong for ordinary shareholders was describable beforehand from its own published accounts. Not predictable — describable. That is a lower bar and a far more useful skill.

01

What the accounts are trying to do

A set of company accounts is an attempt to answer three questions with numbers. Did it make a profit? What does it own and owe? Did real money actually move? One statement answers each, and the rest of this page is those three questions in detail.

Before any of that, one rule governs how the numbers get written down, and almost every confusion about accounting traces back to it.

Accrual accounting: earned, not received

Under accrual accounting, revenue is recorded when it is earned and costs are recorded when they are incurred — not when cash changes hands. A sale you have delivered but not been paid for is revenue today. A bill you have received but not yet paid is a cost today.

Suppose Northwind Coffee signs a $120,000 supply contract in March, delivers coffee steadily through the year, and is paid in a single lump in June. Two entirely different pictures come out of the same events.

The companion rule is the matching principle: a cost belongs in the same period as the revenue it helped produce. If Northwind buys beans in February and sells the coffee in April, the cost of those beans lands in April alongside the sale, not in February when the money left.

Accrual exists for a good reason. Cash-basis accounts lurch about with the timing of payments and tell you very little about whether the business works. Accrual smooths that out and shows you the underlying trade. The price is that somebody has to decide when revenue is earned and how to spread a cost — and those decisions are judgement, which is lesson 07.

Where to find the actual documents

  • The annual report — every listed company publishes one, free, on its investor relations page. In the United States the regulatory version is the 10-K; the quarterly one is the 10-Q.
  • The three statements sit together in the middle, usually across four or five pages, with three years of history side by side.
  • The notes follow them and are longer than everything else combined. They are where the accounting policies live, and they are lesson 08.
  • A fiscal year is not a calendar year. Plenty of companies end theirs in June, or in late January after the Christmas trade has cleared. Compare like periods, and be careful with any year that contains 53 weeks.
Why this matters

Every argument about whether a company is "really" profitable is, underneath, an argument about accrual judgement. That is precisely why the cash flow statement exists — it is the check on the other two, and it is the one that is hardest to dress up.

02

The income statement, line by line

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 01 to open this.

The income statement starts with everything that came in the door over a period and subtracts its way down. Each subtraction has a name, and each leftover has a name. That is the whole document.

What each line is actually telling you

LineWhat it isWhat a change in it says
RevenueEverything sold over the period, whether or not it has been paid forThe business is getting bigger or smaller
Cost of revenueThe direct cost of producing what was sold — materials, manufacturing, hostingInput costs, or the price it can charge, are moving
Gross profitRevenue minus cost of revenueHow much room the product itself leaves
R&DWhat it spends inventing the next thingInvestment in the future, expensed today
SG&ASelling, general and administrative — sales teams, marketing, head officeWhether overheads are growing faster than sales
Operating profitWhat the business itself earns, before financing and taxThe cleanest measure of the trading operation
InterestThe cost of the money it has borrowedHow heavily debt is weighing on the result
Net incomeWhat is left for shareholdersThe bottom line, and the one that feeds EPS

Three details that matter more than they look

  • Operating profit is the honest trading number. It sits above interest and tax, so it describes the business rather than how it happens to be financed or where it is domiciled. Two identical companies with different debt loads show the same operating profit and very different net income.
  • EPS comes in two versions. Basic divides net income by the shares in issue. Diluted divides it by the shares that would exist if every option and convertible were exercised. Diluted is the conservative one, and it is the one to use.
  • Depreciation and amortisation are in here somewhere. Sometimes on their own line, often buried inside cost of revenue and SG&A. They are real costs but no money moves, which is why the cash flow statement adds them straight back — lesson 04.
Why this matters

The three margins answer three different questions. Gross margin is about the product — can it be sold for meaningfully more than it costs to make? Operating margin is about the company — is it disciplined about its own overheads? Net margin is about the whole structure, including debt and tax. A company can be strong on one and weak on another, and knowing which tells you where to look next.

03

The balance sheet, line by line

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 02 to open this.

The income statement covers a stretch of time. The balance sheet is a photograph of one single day — usually the last day of the period — showing what the company controls and who has a claim on it.

One equation holds, always, by construction: assets = liabilities + equity. Everything the company controls was funded either by borrowing it or by the owners. Equity is simply the leftover, which is why it is sometimes called the residual claim.

Current and non-current: the split that does the work

Both sides are divided into current — expected to turn into cash, or fall due, within twelve months — and non-current, meaning everything longer-dated. That single split is what lets you ask the only question that really matters here: if the next year is bad, does this company have a problem?

  • Current assets — cash, receivables (invoices customers have not paid), inventory, prepaid expenses.
  • Non-current assets — property, plant and equipment; intangibles such as patents and software; goodwill; long-term investments.
  • Current liabilities — payables (bills not yet paid), accrued expenses, deferred revenue, and any debt falling due within the year.
  • Non-current liabilities — long-term borrowings, lease obligations, pension deficits.
  • Equity — share capital and share premium (what shareholders originally put in), plus retained earnings: every dollar of profit the company has ever made and not paid out, accumulated since it began.

Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities, and it is the buffer between the two. Its cousin, the current ratio, is the same thing as a division: $150m of current assets against $100m of current liabilities is 1.5. Comfortably above 1 is ordinary; well below 1 for a business that is not collecting cash up front is worth understanding.

Two entries that confuse everybody at first

  • Goodwill is not a measure of reputation. It appears only when a company buys another one and pays more than the fair value of the identifiable net assets it acquired. The excess has to go somewhere, and it goes here. Large goodwill is not a fault, but it is a record of prices paid, and if those acquisitions disappoint it gets written off in one lump — an impairment.
  • Deferred revenue is a liability, which feels backwards. A customer has paid for something not yet delivered, so the company owes them the service. It is generally a good sign: the customer funded the business before receiving anything, which is the opposite of a receivable.
Why this matters

The income statement can look excellent during the year in which a company runs out of room. The balance sheet is the only one of the three that tells you whether a bad year is survivable — how much is owed, how soon, and how much cushion sits behind it.

04

The cash flow statement, line by line

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 03 to open this.

This is the statement that tracks money rather than judgement, and it is organised into three sections: what the business generated, what it spent on long-lived things, and what it did with owners and lenders.

The three sections

SectionWhat sits in itWhat it tells you
OperatingNet income, non-cash add-backs, changes in working capitalWhether the business itself produces money
InvestingCapital expenditure, acquisitions, purchases and sales of securitiesWhat it is spending to stay alive and to grow
FinancingDebt raised or repaid, shares issued or bought back, dividends paidIts dealings with lenders and owners

Why it starts at net income

Almost every company uses the indirect method, which looks strange the first time. Rather than listing money in and money out, it starts with net income from the income statement and then undoes every accrual until only cash remains:

  • Add back non-cash charges. Depreciation, amortisation and stock-based compensation reduced profit but moved no money, so they go straight back on.
  • Adjust for changes in working capital. Receivables up means sales were booked but not collected — subtract. Inventory up means cash went into stock — subtract. Payables up means suppliers are still waiting — add.
  • What is left is cash from operations: the money the trading business actually produced.

Free cash flow is then operating cash flow minus capital expenditure — the money genuinely available to repay debt, buy back shares, pay a dividend or reinvest. It is not a defined line on the statement; you calculate it yourself from two that are.

One caution about capex. Some of it is maintenance — replacing what wears out just to keep going — and some is growth. Companies rarely split the two, and a business whose free cash flow only looks healthy because it has stopped replacing its equipment is borrowing from its own future.

Why this matters

Operating cash flow that persistently trails net income is the single most useful thing an amateur can notice in a set of accounts. It does not prove anything on its own — a fast-growing business consumes cash for perfectly good reasons, which is lesson 06. But it is the question that leads to every other question worth asking.

05

How the three lock together

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 04 to open this.

This is the lesson that turns three documents into one model. The statements are not three separate reports that happen to be printed together — they are three views of the same set of events, and they are wired to each other at four points.

The four links, in words

  • Net income. It is the last line of the income statement, the first line of the cash flow statement, and — after any dividend — the amount added to retained earnings on the balance sheet.
  • Depreciation. It reduces profit on the income statement, is added straight back on the cash flow statement because no money moved, and reduces the carrying value of property and equipment on the balance sheet.
  • Working capital. The year-on-year changes in receivables, inventory and payables on the balance sheet are the working capital adjustments in the operating section.
  • Ending cash. The final line of the cash flow statement is the cash line on the balance sheet. Not similar to it — the same number.

One year, tied together

Take the figures from the last three lessons: net income $16m, depreciation $9m, $5m absorbed by working capital, $12m of capital expenditure, and a $4m dividend. Here is what the balance sheet has to do about it.

Balance sheet lineStartChangeEndWhere the change came from
Cash$30m+$4m$34mThe bottom line of the cash flow statement
Property & equipment$150m+$3m$153m$12m of capex, less $9m of depreciation
Retained earnings$100m+$12m$112m$16m of net income, less the $4m dividend

Notice that not one of those three changes is a number you can invent independently. Each is forced by something on another statement. That is the whole reason the model is difficult to distort for long: a flattering figure in one place has to be paid for somewhere else, and the somewhere else is usually cash.

Why this matters

This is the practical test to run on any set of accounts. Take net income, follow it to the top of the cash flow statement, and see how far apart the two ends are. Then take the ending cash and check it against the balance sheet. If profit is growing and cash is not, the difference is sitting in working capital or in non-cash charges — and the accounts will tell you which, if you ask them in this order.

06

Working capital: where profit and cash separate

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 05 to open this.

Working capital is the reason a profitable company can run out of money. It is not an accounting trick — it is the ordinary consequence of paying for things before your customers pay you.

  • Receivables — you delivered and booked the sale; the customer has not paid. Rising receivables consume cash.
  • Inventory — you spent money on stock that has not been sold. Rising inventory consumes cash.
  • Payables — your suppliers have delivered and you have not paid them. Rising payables release cash: you are using their money.
  • Deferred revenue — customers paid before delivery. Also releases cash, which is why subscription businesses can grow without needing much of it.

The cash conversion cycle

Those three numbers have names, and together they form the cash conversion cycle: days of inventory, plus days of receivables, minus days of payables. Here that is 60 + 45 − 30 = 75 days during which the company's own money is out of the door and has not come back.

The figure itself matters less than its direction. A cycle stretching from 60 days to 90 over three years means the business is funding progressively more, and something is being given away — longer payment terms to win sales, or stock that is not moving as it used to.

Some businesses run a negative cycle. A supermarket sells for cash in days and pays its suppliers in weeks; a subscription business collects a year up front. Those companies are funded by their own customers and suppliers, which is a structural advantage that never shows up on the income statement.

The growth trap

Here is the case that catches people out. A product business is genuinely profitable and growing 40% a year. Every additional sale needs stock bought in advance and gets paid for 45 days late. So every dollar of growth consumes cash before it produces any — and the faster it grows, the wider the hole gets.

That company is not failing. It may be an excellent business. But it must fund the gap from somewhere: existing cash, borrowing, or issuing shares. Growth on the income statement with cash draining on the cash flow statement is the signature, and it is why the two have to be read together rather than one after the other.

Why this matters

This is the honest version of "profit is an opinion, cash is a fact". The gap between them is usually not deception at all — it is working capital doing exactly what working capital does. Knowing that stops you from treating every divergence as a scandal, and lets you concentrate on the ones that have no innocent explanation.

07

The judgement calls

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 06 to open this.

Accrual accounting requires somebody to decide things. Those decisions are legitimate, disclosed, and capable of changing reported profit substantially without a single dollar moving differently. Here are the ones worth knowing.

Depreciation, amortisation and useful life

A machine costing $90m that will last nine years is charged at $10m a year. Decide it will last fifteen and the charge becomes $6m — and reported profit is $4m higher every year, on identical cash. Amortisation is the same idea applied to intangibles such as software and patents. Useful life is an estimate, disclosed in the notes, and comparing it to what competitors assume is a quick and revealing check.

Capitalising versus expensing

When a company spends money building something long-lived, it can either expense it — take the whole hit now — or capitalise it, putting it on the balance sheet as an asset and depreciating it over several years. The cash is identical. The reported profit is not.

Neither treatment is dishonest. The rules permit capitalising development costs once a project meets certain tests, and the tests involve judgement. What is worth noticing is a company that capitalises what its direct competitors expense, and whether that policy changed in a year when profit needed help.

Stock-based compensation

Paying staff in shares is a genuine cost — it is part of what the work cost the company — but no cash leaves. So it reduces profit on the income statement and is then added back on the cash flow statement, exactly like depreciation. The effect is that free cash flow looks better than the economics warrant, while the real cost shows up somewhere else entirely: in a rising share count, diluting every existing shareholder. This is the single most common way a technology company can look far more cash-generative than it is.

Impairments and "one-off" items

An impairment is a company admitting an asset is worth less than the books say — usually goodwill from an acquisition that has not worked. It is non-cash and backward-looking; the money was spent years ago. A single large impairment is information about a past decision. A company that takes one most years is telling you something about how it buys things.

Which leads to adjusted or non-GAAP earnings — management's version of profit, with selected items removed. Sometimes that is fair: a genuine one-off legal settlement really does obscure the trend. Sometimes the same category of cost is excluded as exceptional every year for a decade, at which point it is not exceptional, it is the business. Read what has been excluded, and check whether it is the same list as last year.

Why this matters

None of this is fraud, and treating it as such will just make you cynical about ordinary reporting. These are choices, made within the rules and disclosed in the notes. Your job is only to know which choices were made, whether they are consistent with the industry, and whether they changed at a convenient moment.

08

The notes, and what trouble looks like

Locked — answer all five questions in lesson 07 to open this.

The three statements are the summary. The notes are the terms and conditions, and they are where every judgement from lesson 07 is actually disclosed. They are longer than the statements by a wide margin, and nobody reads all of them — but a handful repay the effort every time.

  • Revenue recognition policy — when the company decides a sale counts. The first note worth reading in any business with long contracts.
  • Segment reporting — revenue and profit broken out by division or region. Frequently reveals that one healthy segment is carrying a struggling one.
  • Debt maturities — how much falls due, and when. A schedule with a large repayment next year is a fact about next year.
  • Leases and other commitments — obligations that are real whether or not they sit on the balance sheet.
  • Share-based payment — how many shares have been promised and at what prices.
  • Contingencies and litigation — what might go wrong that has not yet.
  • Related-party transactions — business done with people connected to the company. Usually innocuous. Occasionally the most important page in the document.

What trouble tends to look like

The pattern below is the classic one, and it is visible from the outside long before anything is announced. Revenue keeps climbing; the unpaid invoices climb faster.

What you seeWhat it might meanThe innocent version
Receivables outgrowing revenueSales booked that customers are slow to pay, or terms loosened to hit targetsA shift towards larger enterprise customers who simply pay slower
Inventory outgrowing revenueStock that is not selling and may need writing downDeliberate stock-building ahead of a launch or a supply squeeze
Cash flow below profit for yearsReported profit is not converting into moneyGenuine growth absorbing working capital, as in lesson 06
Repeated "one-off" chargesAn ordinary cost being presented as exceptionalA long restructuring genuinely executed in stages
Goodwill rising, returns fallingAcquisitions bought growth at prices that have not paid offRecent deals that have not had time to contribute yet
Capitalising what peers expenseProfit flattered by an accounting choiceA genuinely different mix of work that meets the tests

How to use that table honestly

Note that every row has a plausible innocent explanation, and most of the time the innocent explanation is the right one. That is why the third column exists. A single flag is a question, not a verdict.

What carries weight is a pattern: several flags at once, pointing the same way, persisting across years, in a company whose management keeps directing you to an adjusted figure. The disciplined habit is to ask "what would explain this that isn't a problem?" first — and then to notice when you cannot find an answer.

Why this matters

You are not auditing anybody, and you will not detect fraud from a public filing. What you can do is far more useful: describe what the accounts say, notice when the description does not match the story being told about the company, and decline to own the ones where those two things keep diverging. That is a low bar, it costs nothing, and it rules out most of the disasters.
