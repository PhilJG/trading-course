Reading a stock chart, from zero

Twenty minutes, three lessons, no jargon. By the end you'll be able to look at a stock chart and actually say what it's doing.

You said the thing that worries you is: it seems like an easy way to lose money, and there's a lot of hype around it — I don't want to lose a lot of money and get stressed out.

That's the correct instinct, and it's the one this page is built around. Trading is an easy way to lose money — mostly because people act before they can read what's in front of them. So there is nothing to buy here and no signals to follow. This is just learning to read. Reading costs nothing, carries no risk, and is the part the hype always skips.

01

What one candle is telling you

A stock chart is usually drawn as a row of candles. Each candle covers one slice of time — one day on a daily chart, one hour on an hourly chart — and it records four prices from that slice.

Take a made-up example. Apple AAPL on a daily chart. During that one day:

  • Open — the price at the start of the day. Say $180.
  • High — the highest price anyone paid all day. $186.
  • Low — the lowest price anyone paid all day. $178.
  • Close — the price at the end of the day. $184.

The thick part is the body: it stretches from the open to the close. The thin lines poking out of the top and bottom are the wicks — they reach out to the high and the low. If the close is above the open, the candle is drawn green (an up candle). If the close is below the open, it's red (a down candle).

Wicks matter more than beginners expect. A long wick means price went somewhere and got rejected — buyers or sellers pushed it there, and it came straight back. A candle with a long top wick and a small body says "someone tried to push AAPL to $186 and it didn't stick."

Why this answers your worry

Hype talks in stories — "this stock is going to the moon." A candle is not a story. It's four numbers that already happened. Learning to read them is how you replace someone else's excitement with your own observation.

Check 1 — A daily candle for Microsoft MSFT opens at $410, trades as high as $418, dips to $405, and closes at $407. What does that candle look like?

02

Market structure: what the chart is doing

Locked — answer Check 1 correctly to open this.

One candle is a moment. Zoom out and the candles form a shape, and that shape has a name. There are only three, and every stock chart you ever look at is doing one of them.

To see it, ignore the individual candles and look only at the turning points — the peaks (swing highs) and the dips (swing lows).

  • Uptrend — each peak is higher than the last peak, and each dip is higher than the last dip. Higher highs and higher lows. Buyers are in control.
  • Downtrend — each peak is lower than the last, and each dip is lower than the last. Lower highs and lower lows. Sellers are in control.
  • Range — the peaks stop at roughly the same level and the dips stop at roughly the same level. Nobody's in control. Price is going sideways.

Two practical notes. First, structure depends on the timeframe: AAPL can be in an uptrend on the weekly chart and a downtrend on the hourly at the same time, and both are true. Second, a trend doesn't end because it "feels high." It ends when the pattern breaks — an uptrend is over when price makes a lower low, not before.

Why this answers your worry

Most of the money beginners lose is lost buying a stock that's already making lower highs and lower lows, because a headline said it was cheap. Structure gives you one calm question to ask before anything else: which of the three is this? Sometimes the honest answer is "sideways and unclear", and the correct response to that is to do nothing.

03

Support and resistance: floors and ceilings

Locked — answer Check 2 correctly to open this.

Now the last piece. Price doesn't wander randomly — it keeps reacting at the same prices, over and over. Those prices have names.

  • Support is a floor: a price where falls keep stopping, because enough buyers show up there. Say MSFT drops to about $400 three separate times and bounces each time — $400 is support.
  • Resistance is a ceiling: a price where rallies keep stalling, because enough sellers show up there. If MSFT pushes toward $440 and gets knocked back twice, $440 is resistance.

Why do they exist? Nothing mystical. Lots of people remember that price. Some are waiting to buy where they missed out; some are waiting to sell where they broke even. Their orders sit there and price reacts.

Two things to hold on to. Levels are zones, not exact prices — MSFT support at "$400" really means roughly $398–$402. And when a level finally breaks, it often flips role: a ceiling that price climbs above tends to become the floor on the way back down, which is the move drawn on the right of that chart.

Why this answers your worry

This is the piece that most directly protects your money. Levels give you a place where you'd be provably wrong. If you thought $400 was the floor and price closes well below it, the idea was wrong — that's information, not a disaster, and it's a decision you made in advance rather than in a panic. Deciding where you're wrong before you're in is most of the difference between trading and stress.

Check 3 — Amazon AMZN has stalled at roughly $185 four times over two months. It finally closes at $191, then a week later drifts back to $185 and starts pushing up from there. What just happened?

Five questions to finish

1. On a daily candle, what does the body show?

2. A TSLA daily candle has a small body near the bottom and a very long upper wick. What's the most reasonable read?

3. Which pattern of swings defines an uptrend?

4. MSFT has bounced off roughly $400 three times. What is $400 called, and how should you treat it?

5. A stock you like is making lower highs and lower lows, but three people online say it's a screaming buy. What does the chart actually tell you?
